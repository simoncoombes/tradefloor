GUIDE

Realism and Limits

pretium publishes what it gets right and what it gets wrong: ten statistics measured against real markets, how long those results hold for, the roster they were measured on, and six known gaps. Each gap ends with a plain rule about what not to use the simulator for.

Read This Before You Conclude Anything

Any simulator will hand you a number for a question it cannot answer. This page tells you which questions it can, with the measurements behind each claim. Reading it first costs you five minutes. Finding out afterwards that your two-year backtest ran a year past the tested horizon costs you the study.

How to Read the Numbers on This Site

"Measured" is used for figures from several different methods, and the difference matters, so every figure here carries a provenance line naming five things: the statistic, the preset, the seeds, the roster, and the horizon. Three methods recur.

certification panelpt-v3, 30 seeds, 40 instruments, 252 days. The figures in the table below, and the only ones the word "certified" applies to.
six-seed panelpt-v3, sim seeds 1 to 6, Universe.random(40, seed=111), 252 days. What pt.facts reports, and what the release notes quote. Same statistics, fewer seeds, so the values differ slightly from the panel above.
single pinned runOne seed, a smaller roster, 120 days, a macro field held fixed. What the scenario figures come from. Good for comparing one pin against another, and not comparable to either panel.

Output blocks carry a badge. MEASURED means the numbers came from the run named beside the block. ILLUSTRATIVE means the shapes and field names are real and the values were composed, so you can see what a call returns without inheriting a number nobody ran.

Check Your Question First

envelope.check takes your horizon and the statistics your conclusion leans on, and answers with a boolean that carries its reasons. Say you run a quant desk and want to test a two-year vol-targeting strategy that sizes off a one-month volatility estimate:

PYTHON 
from pretium import envelope

verdict = envelope.check(
    horizon_days=504,
    statistics=["abs_return_acf20", "excess_kurtosis"],
)
bool(verdict)      # False
print(verdict)

Each reason points at the measurement behind it, so you can check it yourself. Misspell a statistic and you get an error rather than an answer. If it quietly ignored names it did not recognise, you would walk away thinking you had the all-clear.

Seed-to-Seed Variation

Every number above is the middle of thirty runs, and your one run will not be the middle. Measured on the shipped preset, eight of the ten statistics have their 10th-to-90th percentile range across seeds crossing a band edge. abs_return_acf1 reads a median of 0.141 against a ceiling of 0.22, with a p90 of 0.426 and an across-seed standard deviation of 0.170, larger than the median itself.

So "9/10 in band" describes the middle of thirty runs. Your one run can easily look worse. Read the spread before relying on one seed:

PYTHON 
panels = [pt.facts.measure(seed=s, universe=universe) for s in range(1, 31)]
rows = envelope.intervals(panels)
print(envelope.report_intervals(rows))

Each row reports the median, the p10-p90 range, the across-seed standard deviation, and two containment tests. typical_straddles is the one to read: it says the middle eighty percent of seeds crosses a band edge, so one seed is likely to read out of band on a statistic whose median sits comfortably inside.

Two Warnings Worth Repeating

One Seed Is One Sample

A verdict from a single run measures the seed as much as the thing you are testing. Every number on this page is the middle of thirty runs, and eight of the ten statistics have their middle-eighty-percent range crossing a band edge. Use pt.rank across seeds for comparisons, and envelope.intervals for statistics.

Simulated Returns Are Not a Forecast

Prices here come from a model, so a strategy that fits the model's structure looks brilliant and teaches you nothing. This is not a gap in this model; it is true of every market simulator, and no amount of realism work changes it. Read the failures, not the P&L.

Scoring a Panel You Measured Yourself

Two functions turn the judgement call into a check.

envelope.score(panel, horizon_days=...)How your measured panel sits against the bands for its own horizon, including room_sd: how far inside a statistic sits, in that horizon's own seed noise. Scoring a 504-day panel against the 252-day bands is the wrong-ruler error, and this exists partly to make that hard to commit.
envelope.regressions(panel)The statistics the shipped preset holds in band and your panel does not. An empty list means your candidate certifies at least as well as what ships.

The policy those encode is worth stating outright: a candidate that regresses the certified horizon does not become the default, whatever the calibration objective says. The objective is the search's proxy; the envelope is the contract. That is exactly why pt-v4 ships selectable rather than default.

Known Gaps

Each of these was measured, and each one says what it rules out. Note that the horizon gaps only bite past a year.

1 · The tenth statistic is structurally unreachable

volume_change_acf1 reads −0.46 against a real band of −0.32 to −0.20: 13.7 seed-standard-deviations out. A held volume level plus independent per-tick noise sits near −0.5 at any coefficients, which means no parameter setting can reach it. It is excluded from the calibration objective deliberately, because an optimiser pointed at an unreachable target distorts every other parameter chasing it. It stays in every report as a standing falsification verdict.

Forbids: strategies trading the day-to-day change in volume. The volume level's relationship to volatility is in band and fine to use. A volume forecast here is never wrong twice running, so an execution algorithm tested against this market faces an easier problem than the one it was written for.

2 · The certified horizon is 252 days

Measured against bands re-derived at the matching 504-day window, the model holds 5 of 10. Volatility clustering roughly doubles from 252 to 504 days where real markets move about 14%. The price level stays plausible, so long runs look fine and are not. The dynamics leave the envelope while the chart still looks like a market.

Forbids: multi-year backtests, and anything keyed on volatility dynamics beyond one year.

3 · Volatility memory has the wrong shape

Real markets' volatility autocorrelation decays like a power law. The model's decays exponentially, because it is built from exponentials: per-name GARCH plus an AR(1) factor variance. Over one year two exponentials fake a power law well enough that no panel statistic objects. The fake thins out as the window grows, and by lag 30 the model's memory changes sign. Where a real market still says yesterday's turbulence makes today's more likely a month later, this one says the mild opposite.

On log-log axes real markets fit a slope of −0.436 over lags 1-20. The model fits −0.953, about 2.2 times steeper. This is a mechanism gap: no parameter setting turns one slope into the other. It was tried, and a two-component variance mixture lands lag 20 while getting lag 60 wrong in both directions at once.

Forbids: strategies whose edge depends on volatility memory beyond about lag 20. Vol targeting and risk parity on a one-month or longer estimate are outside the envelope.

4 · Tails are too thin over multi-year windows

Over 504-day windows real markets show excess kurtosis of 7.1 to 22. The model shows 5.2. The 252-day band's floor of 1.6 is wide enough that this reads as comfortably in band on every 252-day certificate, which is why it went unnoticed: nothing was measuring kurtosis where it fails.

Forbids: tail-risk or VaR calibration at multi-year horizons. At the certified 252-day horizon, kurtosis is in band.

5 · Scenario response is directional, not calibrated

Two quantities, failing differently. The steady state is the ratio of realised annualised volatility at crisis VIX to realised annualised volatility at calm VIX, measured on the 40-name reference roster: about 3.1 times here against real markets' 6.16 (17.2% annualised below VIX 12 against 106.1% above VIX 45). Roughly half. That is not the same quantity as the 82% against 62% in a single pinned 120-day run elsewhere on this site, which is one seed on a smaller roster over a shorter window. The transient: the shipped preset retains 27.6% of the previous preset's shock response, because it raised factor-variance persistence to 0.989 to buy volatility clustering, and a 63-day half-life cannot track a twenty-day spike.

This was attacked directly and the attack failed, which is why it is a gap and no longer a task. A two-timescale variance mixture restored the transient (1.062 to 1.203) and doubled the 504-day loss (0.9887 to 2.0164), and raising the slow component's weight made the horizon monotonically worse while the transient stayed flat. Within this model class, restoring the crisis transient costs long-horizon realism.

Forbids: sizing a scenario's impact rather than detecting it. Use scenarios to ask whether a strategy breaks. A crisis here is about half as violent as a real one and arrives more slowly, so surviving one is a weaker test than the label suggests.

6 · Certification used a sector-balanced roster

Universe.random() places exactly five names in each of twelve sectors. No real index is balanced that way. The S&P is roughly a third technology, and the Nasdaq more so. Varying only sector composition:

Why There Is No Realism Score

A model is realistic in some ways and at some scales, not others. One number would hide the detail that decides whether your result means anything. There is also a practical problem: numbers get quoted and warnings do not. "87% realistic" travels. "Volatility clustering runs about twice real beyond one year" does not. So you get a yes or no with the reasons attached, and nothing here is called a realism score.

What to Use It For

USE PRETIUM FOR
  • Strategy evaluation up to about one year, where the edge depends on statistics the panel certifies
  • Relative comparison of strategies under identical conditions: same seed, differences come from the strategies
  • Testing whether a strategy survives a regime change or a stress scenario at all
  • Agent and RL environments that need a plausible, self-consistent market
DO NOT USE IT FOR
  • Multi-year backtests (gap 2)
  • Sizing how badly a crisis hurts, as opposed to detecting that it does (gap 5)
  • Inheriting these numbers on a sector-concentrated roster (gap 6)
  • Long-horizon volatility memory (gap 3), or VaR at multi-year horizons (gap 4)
  • Strategies trading the change in volume (gap 1)
  • Any claim that simulated performance forecasts live returns

Machine-readable companion: envelope.json carries the per-statistic detail, the gap list and the provenance. Both measurement runs report known-answer digest 992ef95d…dc185e3. If the digest in envelope.json does not match your installed wheel, this page describes a different model than the one you are running.

Checking a Study Before Running It

The whole sequence for a six-month momentum study: check the question, measure the panel on your own universe, read the seed spread, then run the study.

PYTHON 
import pretium as pt
from pretium import envelope

u = pt.Universe.random(40, seed=111)

# 1. is this a question the simulator can answer?
verdict = envelope.check(
    horizon_days=126,
    statistics=["return_acf1", "abs_return_acf1"],
)
print(bool(verdict))
print(verdict)

# 2. the warnings above point here: measure the spread yourself
panels = [pt.facts.measure(seed=s, universe=u, days=126)
          for s in range(1, 31)]
rows = envelope.intervals(panels)
print(envelope.report_intervals(rows))

# 3. only now run the study
spec = pt.StrategySpec.momentum(lookback_days=1.0, top_k=5)
scores = pt.evaluate({"mom": spec}, seed=7, universe=u, days=126)
print(scores["mom"].return_pct, scores["mom"].impact_bps)
